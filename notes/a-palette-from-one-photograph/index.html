A palette from one photograph

21.06.20268 min read

Colour pickers make it easy to grab five swatches from a photo. They rarely give you a palette you can actually design with. Here's the slower method I use.

1. Pick the photograph for its light, not its colours

A good source image has a clear light condition — overcast, late afternoon, fluorescent. Light is what makes colours feel related. A photo with three light sources will give you three palettes fighting each other.

2. Find the neutral first

Before any accent, find the colour that will carry most of the page: usually a shadow or a wall. Sample it in a few places and average by eye. This becomes your background or your text colour, and everything else has to work against it.

3. Take one accent, and only one

The temptation is to keep every interesting colour. Resist it. Choose the one that's most specific to this image — the thing you'd remember about it — and keep the rest as supporting tones, desaturated towards the neutral.

Where it goes wrong

  • Contrast. Photo colours are rarely dark or light enough for text. Push the neutral further than feels natural and check it properly.
  • Screens. A colour sampled from a photo looks different as a flat field. Test it at full size before committing.
  • Too many tones. If you can't name what each colour is for, there are too many.

The result should feel like the photograph without needing it. That's how you know the palette works on its own.
